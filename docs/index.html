Speak as you think

In my tests on English text, Drama 3's own streaming-text format cut the median gap where a reply is cut mid-phrase from 0.45 s to 0.14 s. Keeping the voice stable costs part of that: with one round of memory the gap is 0.25 s.

Hear it happen: the live demo, replayed from recorded runs. One reply spoken three ways, the gap at each cut measured as it plays, and an interrupt button.

The short version

  • A voice agent starts speaking before the LLM has finished its sentence, so the text is cut into small chunks. With one request per chunk, a chunk that ends mid-phrase is followed by a gap: a median 0.45 s of non-speech at the cut.
  • Drama 3's tech report lists streaming text input as new: each round holds the chunk to speak plus the next chunk as lookahead. None of the open engines I checked (fish-speech, SGLang-Omni, vLLM-Omni) builds these rounds. Fish Audio's hosted services do run Drama 3 in realtime, as a closed service.
  • I ran the format through fish-speech on text cut inside sentences. With every earlier round in memory, the median gap at a cut with no punctuation is 0.14 s; fish-speech's history context on the same cuts leaves 0.41 s.
  • The cost is the voice: with all rounds in memory it drifts from the reference. Keeping only the last round holds the voice and leaves a 0.25 s gap. No setting gives both yet.
  • On one L40 the model decodes at about twice real time, so a live session is within reach. The quality claim is about pause timing; the only listening so far is my own informal blind check.

The numbers

A seam is where one chunk's audio ends and the next begins. Each bar is the median non-speech there: the trailing non-speech of one chunk plus the leading non-speech of the next, by Silero VAD.

Lists of four numbers below are in the order Independent chunks / fish-speech history / Streaming, last round / Streaming, all rounds.

  • With every round in memory, the gap inside a phrase shrinks to a third. Where the cut has no punctuation, both baselines leave almost half a second. Streaming with all rounds had less non-speech than Independent chunks at 117 of 138 of those cuts; with only the last round, at 87 of 138. Share of those cuts over 0.3 s: 78% / 69% / 43% / 25%.
  • The pause follows the punctuation. Streaming with all rounds: 0.14 s inside a phrase, 0.64 s at a comma, 0.86 s at a sentence end. At sentence ends it did not pause less: more non-speech than Independent chunks at 34 of 60 cuts, less at 24 (English and Chinese together).
  • Lookahead and history both count. With lookahead and one round of history the median is 0.25 s. fish-speech history, which keeps the first and the latest chunk and has no lookahead, leaves 0.41 s. More rounds help further: 0.16 s with the last four, 0.14 s with all.
  • Medians, because chunks can fail. One Independent chunk never stopped: after the fragment “in time. The doors slid open with” it produced 27 s of non-speech up to the token limit (1 of 264 chunks). It moves the mid-phrase mean: 0.72 / 0.49 / 0.36 / 0.21 s with it, and 0.53 / 0.48 / 0.37 / 0.21 s when that text's seed-1 run is left out in every mode. One streaming chunk with four rounds of memory ran long too: 17.5 s for “morning. Outside, the city slipped”. Both chunks begin with the tail of one sentence and end inside the next.

Memory trades the gap against the voice

Speaker similarity to the reference (ECAPA), over whole clips and from the first 6 seconds of a clip to the last 6.

  • With all rounds in memory the voice moves away from the reference over a reply. Likely cause: the whole history is in context, so the reference weighs less over time.
  • Keeping only the last round holds the voice: higher similarity than the all-rounds mode on all 18 clips, and close to Independent chunks. It gives back part of the gap.
  • Keeping the last four rounds keeps the short gap and does not stop the drift.
  • No setting gives both yet. Pinning the first spoken round next to the latest one, as fish-speech's history does, is the next thing to try.

Checks

  • I transcribed every chunk: the lookahead was spoken early in 0% of them, in every streaming mode. Repeated words were not measured on their own.
  • English word error rate over whole replies (Whisper large-v3): 1.7% / 0.9% / 3.4% / 0.4%. The 1.7% includes the failed chunk. The 3.4% comes from two last-round clips (18.8% and 14.5%) whose chunks, transcribed one by one, are at 1.0% and 3.6%; I have not yet found out whether the recogniser or the audio is at fault. Chunk by chunk, the English error is 3.2% / 2.2% / 2.1% / 0.8%.
  • Predicted naturalness (UTMOSv2) was scored for the three round-2 modes only and is a tie: 3.33 Independent chunks, 3.37 fish-speech history, 3.42 Streaming with all rounds, which scored higher than Independent chunks on 9 of 18 clips.

Speed

Measured during round 3 on one L40 (44 GB, FP8 experts, CUDA graphs on): the model decodes 44.8 frames per second, where real time needs 21.5. The offline harness re-reads the reference and the remembered rounds for every chunk (about 620 prompt rows, 0.56 s) and still ran at RTF 0.75 over 7 runs: a median 2.0 s to produce a 2.8 s chunk.

  • Split across two L40s, fish-speech turns its CUDA graphs off and decoding falls to 10.6 frames per second (RTF 2.3). The A40 and A100 runs of rounds 1 and 2 were also split across cards, in bf16, and their timings say nothing about the model.
  • The single card ran out of memory when the harness decoded a whole reply's waveform at once, so the remaining runs used two cards. A live engine decodes a short window at a time.
  • Published figures for ordinary single-turn requests are higher still (SGLang-Omni cookbook, one H200, one stream, pre-encoded reference: mean RTF 0.131, median first audio 88.9 ms).

Limits

  • Two hardware setups. The two short-memory modes ran on L40 cards with FP8 experts; the other three ran on A40 and A100 cards in bf16. Same weights, different arithmetic. A control run of every mode on the same card is pending, so comparisons between the short-memory modes and the rest carry that caveat.
  • The first cut of a reply gets no short pause. At the first cut of a text, streaming left a median 0.72 s with all rounds and with the last round alike (9 English mid-phrase cuts: 3 texts, 3 seeds), against 0.13 s and 0.22 s from the second cut on. Against Independent chunks the first cut is mixed: a higher median (0.72 s against 0.48 s), yet with all rounds shorter at 6 of the 9 cuts.
  • Pause timing, plus one informal listen. I listened blind to 8 randomly drawn mid-phrase cuts from round 2, three modes in random order, and picked the one that sounded least broken: Streaming with all rounds 4 times, fish-speech history 3, Independent chunks 1. My pick was the mode with the shortest measured gap at 4 of the 8. One listener who knows the project is not a listening test, and pause length is not the same as sentence-ending prosody.
  • Small test. One voice (my own), six texts, three seeds. The Chinese texts were cut at punctuation, so the mid-phrase effect is tested in English only.
  • Lookahead costs text. A chunk can be spoken only once the next chunk has arrived from the LLM. What that adds to time to first audio is not measured yet.

How it works

The format is Figure 10 in Appendix A of the tech report. Each round, the user turn holds the chunk to speak, the token <|endofturn|>, and the next chunk as lookahead; the assistant turn is the audio of the current chunk. Earlier rounds stay in context, and an empty lookahead marks the last round.

system     streaming speech continuation with one-chunk lookahead split by <|endofturn|>
user       [reference transcript]<|endofturn|>The train was already moving when
assistant  [reference audio]
user       The train was already moving when<|endofturn|>Anna reached the platform, her
assistant  [audio of “The train was already moving when”]
user       Anna reached the platform, her<|endofturn|>bag bouncing against her hip.
assistant  [audio of “Anna reached the platform, her”]

Schematic: chat-template tokens are left out. Two things are my choice and not in Figure 10: a <|speaker:0|> tag at the start of each user turn, as fish-speech writes its own turns, and the reference clip as round 0, which in round 1 stayed closer to the reference voice than a reference in the system prompt.

  • Test. Six texts in the style of agent replies and short stories (4 English, 2 Chinese), cut into chunks of about 30 characters in English and 14 in Chinese so that most cuts fall inside a sentence. Per run: 46 mid-phrase cuts, 16 at a comma, 20 at a sentence end. Three seeds, 264 chunks per mode.
  • Independent chunks. Each chunk synthesized alone with the reference, as a chunk-per-request cascade does.
  • fish-speech history. fish-speech's long-form context rule on the same cuts: the first and the latest chunk, no lookahead. On its own, fish-speech cuts at about 300 bytes and prefers sentence ends.
  • Streaming. The format above with every earlier round in context, or with only the last round or the last four besides the reference.
  • Harness. About 1,200 lines of Python around fish-speech, with unit tests on the pure-Python parts. One script rebuilds the pause numbers and every clip on this page from the raw audio; error rates and speaker similarity come from the scoring step, and UTMOS was scored separately.

Has this been measured before?

The tech report defines the format and lists streaming text input as new. Its streaming evaluation (Section 5.4) covers streamed audio output, one sentence per request. I found no measurement of the streaming-text format in it.

SGLang-Omni's roadmap (issue 2994, item B8) asks to evaluate a small text-lookahead window, to verify that lookahead text is “neither spoken early nor repeated”, to measure sentence-ending prosody, and to document whether the capability already exists or requires model changes. Pull request 3318 (open) tested lookahead on a different model, Qwen3-TTS Base, at sentence boundaries and reported: “The only thing lookahead changed was the pause between sentences.” Pull request 3263 (open) ran Drama 3 at its default flags, one request per sentence, found it behind Qwen3-TTS Base on intelligibility, sentence-to-sentence voice consistency and speed, and proposes keeping Qwen3-TTS Base for the cascade.

Neither ran Drama 3 in its own streaming-text format with cuts inside sentences. This page is that arm. On B8's last question: the capability is in the model, and what is missing is an engine that keeps the rounds. The consistency concern holds here too: voice stability is where this mode has to be tuned.

What is next

A session engine for the mode: keep the model's caches between rounds instead of re-reading the context per chunk, stream audio frame by frame, and on interruption roll the context back to the last frame the listener heard. Then a WebSocket protocol, a TypeScript/React client, and a recorded live demo: LLM text in, speech out, one interruption, latency on screen.
